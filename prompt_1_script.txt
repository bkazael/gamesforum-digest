ARTICLE 7: In-game vs. web store: How to price and package for D2C without breaking your game economy  [airtime: ~5% of the episode, about 92 words]
URL: https://www.pocketgamer.biz/in-game-vs-web-store-how-to-price-and-package-for-d2c-without-breaking-your-game-economy/

Chip Thurston, FastSpring's head of gaming, breaks down how publishers should think about pricing, packaging and incentives when going D2C, and why your game economy has to come first

 By Guest Author (Sponsored) 

 October 2, 2026 

 Share:

 Securing that first web store purchase is the hardest challenge in D2C monetisation.

 Additional entitlements consistently outperform price discounts.

 Direct checkout removes the steps that players don’t want to deal with, and conversion rates show it works.

 There’s no universal D2C pricing playbook, but there are proven principles to build from.

 Only 2% to 3% of players will ever spend money in many mobile games and getting that first purchase is the hardest challenge in game monetisation. It gets even harder when you’re asking a player to step outside of the app to make it. 

 That tension sits at the heart of a direct-to-consumer (D2C) pricing strategy. Publishers who manage it well build thriving web stores. Those who don’t either leave money on the table or, worse, destabilize the in-game economies they’ve spent years crafting. 

 Start with economy management, not the price point 

 Most publishers start building their web store offer by asking what price to charge. But the better question is about what their economy can actually support . 

 A game’s economy runs on sources and sinks. Sources are anything the game gives to players: rewards, currency, loot. Sinks are anything that consumes those resources: hard levels, gear upgrades, consumables. Every pricing and packaging decision exists within that balance and it’s worth getting a clear picture of that balance before setting up any D2C offers. 

 Here’s why it matters: If half of a publisher’s revenue flows through a D2C channel offering 10% more per purchase, the overall economy is absorbing 5% more currency than it was before. 

 For games with elastic economies such as social casino games, that additional sourcing is manageable. For mid-core games with tighter mechanics, it demands more deliberate planning. The goal isn’t to avoid D2C incentives but to make sure the economy has enough sink capacity to keep pace with them. 

 More entitlements vs. lower prices: What the data says 

 The most common question publishers face when designing their web store offerings is whether to give players more for the same price or the same for less. An A/B test comparing both approaches found that additional entitlements consistently outperformed discounts across the metrics that matter: conversion from mobile to web store, total revenue, and new D2C customer creation. The discounts didn’t drive the proportional lift needed to justify the lost margin. 

 The psychology behind it makes sense. When a player sees that $10 buys 100 gems in the app but 110 gems on the web store, the value comparison is immediate. When the price drops from $10 to $9 for the same 100 gems, the math is harder and the perceived benefit is murkier, even if the actual value is the same. 

 “ Most publishers start building their web store offer by asking what price to charge. But the better question is about what their economy can actually support .” Chip Thurston 

 There’s also a straightforward revenue consideration. Offering a discount means taking in less per transaction. Additional entitlements hold the revenue per purchase steady while still giving the player a real reason to make the journey off platform. For most games, entitlements are the stronger lever, as long as the game’s economy can handle the additional sourcing. 

 Of course, this is only one A/B test from one game, not a universal truth. Each game is unique. What’s most important is considering all of the factors surrounding your game’s economy and player base to provide the appropriate D2C incentive. 

 Reducing friction w ith direct checkout 

 Beyond pricing, the structure of the purchase journey itself has a meaningful impact on D2C conversion. The traditional web store flow has multiple handoffs: 

 Tap a button in game . 

 Land on a web store or product catalogue page. 

 Add to cart . 

 Proceed to checkout. 

 Complete the purchase. 

 Return to the game. 

 Each step is an opportunity for a player to drop off. 

 Direct checkout streamlines that journey significantly: 

 Tap on a specific item in game . 

 Land directly on a web-hosted checkout page for that item. 

 Complete the purchase. 

 Return to the game. 

 Players who have already decided what they want to buy don’t need to waste time browsing - they need a fast, frictionless path to completing the transaction. Across FastSpring ’s larger clients, return purchase rates on web stores reach around 90% , a figure that reflects what happens once the habit of D2C purchasing takes hold. 

 One caveat worth flagging: The regulatory environment around steering is moving fast. As steering fees continue to roll out globally , the appeal of direct checkout vs web store checkout will vary from one region to another. Publishers need to stay current with the rules in each territory they operate in. 

 How to drive D2C behaviour without touching your prices 

 Not every game needs to offer more entitlements or cheaper prices to run a successful web store. For publishers who want to maintain pricing parity between in-app and web store - whether to simplify economy management, protect platform relationships or both - there are proven paths to driving D2C behaviour without changing the offer itself. 

 At FastSpring, we found that several triple-A game publishers monetising games on our platform incorporate two key strategies. 

 The first is a content creator program. By integrating a creator code into the web store, publishers give players a reason to purchase there that has nothing to do with price. They can easily support a creator they follow, because a percentage of what the players buy for themselves goes to that creator. 

 The player benefits, the creator benefits and the publisher builds D2C volume without touching their pricing structure. It creates a flywheel of awareness and engagement that keeps running. 

 The second is a loyalty currency. Purchases made on the web store earn a separate currency - loyalty points, VIP credits, exclusive gems - that can only be redeemed back in the web store. 

 The mechanic creates a purchase-and-return loop contained entirely within the D2C channel. Players have a tangible reason to keep coming back and the behaviour compounds over time. 

 Exclusive products as a strategic tool 

 Beyond pricing and incentives, product exclusivity is one of the most effective tools for driving web store behaviour. A subscription tier, a cosmetic item or a content bundle that only exists on the web store gives players who want it no other option than to engage with the D2C channel directly. 

 The product has to earn its exclusivity - meaning that the value has to be real and apparent. But when it lands, it creates a permanent revenue driver tied specifically to the direct channel. 

 “P roduct exclusivity is one of the most effective tools for driving web store behaviour. ” Chip Thurston 

 One example we’ve seen work: a subscription created specifically for a web store, unavailable inside the mobile game, became a meaningful and recurring revenue stream on its own. 

 FastSpring’s subscription management tools are built to support exactly this and publishers that use FastSpring can create and manage web store-exclusive subscription tiers that are entirely separate from in-app purchase flows, with full control over pricing, renewal logic and player communication. 

 Player sentiment around this has shifted considerably too . What felt unfamiliar or inconvenient to players a few years ago is now routine. Web stores have become standard across the major titles, and players broadly accept that some products live on different platforms. 

 The only universal rule: Nothing is universal 

 The most important thing to understand about using a D2C pricing strategy is that there’s no universal playbook. The right incentive level, product exclusivity strategy, checkout structure, etc., will all vary - not just between genres, but between individual titles within the same genre. 

 What does work is forming a clear hypothesis based on the game and its player base, rolling it out, measuring it against the right metrics and iterating quickly.

ARTICLE 8: Playable Ads in 2027: AI, Creative Strategy & Mobile UA  [airtime: ~5% of the episode, about 92 words]
URL: https://www.globalgamesforum.com/news-media/playable-ads-playbook-for-2027-ai-depth-and-the-race-for-creative-scale

By Nicole Lumley 1 October 2026 

 In the not too distant past, playable ads were an experiment that studios would put a portion of their budget behind. This has now changed dramatically. 

 Playable ads are becoming an increasingly important part of mobile games user acquisition, but as production becomes faster and more accessible, the challenge is shifting from simply making playables to making playables that deliver meaningful player experiences.

 That was a key theme at Gamesforum London 2026, where industry experts from Zynga, Kwalee, BoomBit, Reactionional Music and Segwise discussed how playable advertising is evolving, the role AI is playing in production, and what studios need to consider as they look towards 2027.

 Playables are moving from experiment to core UA strategy

 The adoption of playable ads has grown significantly in recent years. As competition increases and advertising platforms demand greater creative variety, studios are under pressure to produce more assets while maintaining performance.

 For Robert Hoeft, Head of Playable Ads at BoomBit, the question has already shifted.

 “Two years ago, the question was more like if we should use playables. Now it's more like how we should do it effectively and efficiently. In terms of both performance and costs.”

 Brijesh Bharadwaj, Co Founder of Segwise, highlighted just how quickly production volumes are increasing. According to data discussed during the panel, the proportion of new creatives produced as playables has risen substantially. 

 Brijesh highlighted that in the last four quarters of 2025, playables accounted for 10-15% of new creatives produced by studios using the format. That figure has since risen to 30% representing a 2-3x increase in just six months. 

 Additionally, production volumes have also increased significantly. Studios producing playables were creating an average of around 17 per quarter; that figure has now risen to 71. This is more than four times more than previously. 

 Looking at the broader picture however, only 13 to 15% of gaming studios are using playables. For smaller studios in particular, the cost and complexity of playable production can remain a barrier.

 Playable ads require a combination of design, art and technical development, while assets must also work across different advertising networks. That complexity has historically pushed many studios towards specialist vendors.

 The pressures are getting harder. Not only on a budget level but logistically, studios can find this challenging as well. 

 So, how can we challenge this?

 AI is lowering the barrier to playable production

 AI was arguably the biggest recurring theme of the discussion and its impact clearly varies studio to studio.

 For Adam Watts, Playable Designer at Kwalee, AI is already helping bring playable development further in-house.

 “I think there's obviously still challenges... But I think once you've got into the rhythm of it and you've got a few successful playables under your belt, it's easier to build onto them and create new creative tests.”

 The panel agreed that AI can increase production speed and volume, but there was an important distinction between using AI to make more playables and using it to make better playables.

 AI can help developers prototype ideas, create variations and produce more complex effects in less time. But creative strategy, game knowledge and an understanding of the player remain critical.

 The technology may make production more efficient, but the creative thinking behind a successful playable cannot simply be automated away.

 How much gameplay does a playable really need?

 One of the biggest questions facing playable designers is how deep the experience needs to be.

 The answer from the panel was simple: it depends on the game.

 A playable does not necessarily need to recreate an entire game. Instead, it needs to deliver the core experience or “joy” of the product quickly enough for a player to understand what makes it worth downloading.

 Robert Hoeft stated: ‘’It's never a simple answer because we've seen success with very long playables…But on the other hand, we've also seen very successful case studies with, for example, integral and much shorter narrative driven generated video playables where users can enjoy a story. It's more hook based and it still performs really well, but it's very game dependent.’’

 For a match game, that moment might arrive after a single move. For a more complex puzzle or strategy experience, it could take longer.

 The important point is that players need to experience the fundamental appeal of the game before being asked to install it.

 Making a playable too simple can therefore be just as damaging as making it too complicated. If players do not experience the core appeal, the playable may generate installs without properly qualifying those users.

 Authenticity matters more than ever

 The panel also discussed the evolution away from the novelty-driven playable ads that once dominated mobile advertising.

 Players are increasingly familiar with playable formats, meaning studios need to offer experiences that feel entertaining, authentic and connected to the actual game.

 Adam Watts explained his experience in creating a new playable, and saw that one-click playables were performing well. However when replicated it did not give the same results. He saw that one-click concepts can sometimes go too far in simplifying the experience.

 “There wasn't enough time for the player to actually build a meaningful connection with the experience to then want to go ahead and download it.”

 That connection is particularly important when considering retention.

 A playable should set an expectation that the actual game can fulfil. If the advertising experience promises something fundamentally different, the result may simply be churn after installation rather than genuine player acquisition.

 As Robert Hoeft put it: ‘’The promise made in the playable ad must be matched in the product.” 

 Playable analytics are becoming more sophisticated

 As playable production scales, measurement is also changing. Studios are increasingly able to analyse what happens inside the playable rather than simply looking at the final install or campaign-level results.

 That opens up the possibility of breaking a playable into individual components: the hook, headline, opening move, mechanics, objects, number of interactions and other creative variables.

 Instead of asking whether one playable worked, teams can start asking why it worked.

 Brijesh described this as moving towards a “Lego block” approach to creative analysis, where individual elements can be compared against performance data.

 This could make playable optimisation increasingly similar to established video creative workflows. Rather than producing a playable and starting again from scratch, studios can identify which components are contributing to performance and rapidly produce new iterations around those findings.

 Network differences remain important, however. A playable that performs strongly on one advertising network may not necessarily produce the same results elsewhere, making cross-network testing and benchmarking essential.

 Designing for iteration from the beginning

 One of the most interesting ideas from the panel was that studios should think about experimentation before production begins.

 Rather than creating a finished playable and then trying to work out what worked, teams can build playables around predefined variables that can be changed quickly. These might include the opening mechanic, headline, background, objects or different hooks.

 The result is a more modular approach to production, allowing teams to respond quickly when performance data reveals a promising direction.

 This is particularly powerful for hybrid-casual games, where individual creative elements can be changed without rebuilding the entire experience.

 The approach also reflects a broader shift in mobile UA: creative production is becoming increasingly data-driven, iterative and modular.

 Scaling playables without losing creativity

 As production volumes rise, studios face another challenge: how to scale without creating an endless stream of variations that add volume but little creative value.

 Modular production is one solution. Studios can take successful concepts and change elements such as introductions, end cards, characters, themes or seasonal assets to create additional iterations.

 However, the panel also highlighted the importance of knowing when to use external vendors and when to keep production in-house.

 Craig Smith, Art Director at Zynga highlighted: ‘We're fortunate to have vendors with the customising tools to give video intros and tweak in cards and reskin assets for seasonality or themes or whatever it may be.So you can really maximise your volume out of that, which in the last quarter or two has been essential to us.’’

 Vendors can help studios scale proven concepts and increase production capacity, while internal teams can be better positioned to experiment with completely new ideas and draw on their deeper understanding of the game.

 Rather than viewing the two approaches as mutually exclusive, the panel suggested that a combination of internal and external production can provide both scale and creative diversity.

 The next phase of AI: from volume to experimentation

 The panel's discussion of AI ultimately pointed towards two different creative pipelines. One is focused on speed, automation and volume: rapidly responding to performance data and producing iterations based on what has already been shown to work.

 The other is focused on genuinely new ideas.

 As Brijesh explained, AI can be particularly useful when teams give systems a clear understanding of what constitutes an acceptable creative output.

 “You’re transferring context... like you’re basically treating this system like a new hire who you’re bringing up to speed on what you already know.”

 This could allow AI systems to work within a studio's existing creative guidelines while exploring a much larger number of possible combinations and variations.

 At the same time, human creative teams remain important for developing ideas that do not yet exist in the data. That distinction could become increasingly important as AI makes playable production more accessible.

 Robert Hoeft states: ‘’Now with AI, experienced teams can be really empowered to to quickly prototype some some new playable ideas and and run initial tests.’’

 If everyone has access to the same production tools, simply being able to produce playables quickly will become less of a differentiator.

 What will playable ads look like in 2027?

 Playables could become more personalised, with branching experiences allowing users to take different paths based on their interactions.

 There was also discussion of playables becoming increasingly close to the actual game experience, potentially reducing the distinction between an advertisement and a playable slice of the product.

 For ad networks, better use of behavioural data could also be an important development. The interactions taking place inside a playable provide valuable signals about a potential player's interests and behaviour, but the panel suggested there is still significant room for that data to be better utilised.

 Ultimately, the biggest change may not be the playable format itself, but how studios think about it.

 Playable ads are moving beyond being an additional creative format and towards becoming a sophisticated testing environment for gameplay, creative concepts and player behaviour.

 As production becomes cheaper and AI accelerates iteration, the competitive advantage will increasingly come from knowing what to test, why to test it and how to turn the resulting data into the next creative idea .

 And while AI may make it easier than ever to create a playable, the panel's message was clear: making a playable is becoming easier. Making one that players genuinely want to play is still the real challenge.
